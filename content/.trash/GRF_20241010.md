### Background

LLMs excel in processing vast amounts of unstructured clinical data, which is abundant in electronic health records and medical literature, helping healthcare professionals navigate complex data to make informed decisions. OpenAI's latest model [ChatGPT-o1](2024 A Preliminary Study of o1 in Medicine: Are We Closer to an AI Doctor) achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of medical benchmarks. Additionally, concerns around data privacy and the high cost of proprietary models have driven interest in open-source LLMs, which offer more control over sensitive medical data and lower costs. However, the performance of open-source LLMs continues to lag behind their commercial counterparts, highlighting the need for ongoing advancements to close this gap.
Two key approaches to improving the performance of LLMs are fine-tuning and prompt-engineering. Fine-tuning involves training the model on domain-specific datasets, while prompt-engineering focuses on crafting prompts that guide the model to produce more relevant outputs. Both approaches are essential to optimizing LLMs for complex medical applications. However, to design more effective fine-tuning and prompt-engineering techniques, researchers need a deeper understanding of the underlying behaviors and knowledge learned by these models. Beyond reasoning and domain expertise, LLMs implemented in larger applications must be evaluated for additional skills like instruction-following, function-calling, retrieval-augmented generation, and resolving knowledge conflicts. Benchmarks are essential for assessing LLMs' overall performance as well as testing the models' diverse capabilities.
Numerous benchmarks have been developed to assess LLM performance across various medical tasks. For instance, MultiMedQA integrates several large datasets, including PubMedQA, MedQA, MedMCQA, and MMLU. MedQA, a large-scale benchmark with 15,908 USMLE-style questions, tests LLMs in areas such as pathology, pharmacology, and anatomy. MedMCQA, featuring 194,000 questions, aims to evaluate LLMs in medical contexts by simulating real-world clinical reasoning. While these benchmarks provide an effective means to evaluate the ability of LLMs in addressing real-world medical tasks, they present significant limitations.
For instance, multiple-choice question benchmarks, like those in MedQA, allow for easy performance evaluation but fail to capture the reasoning behind LLM decisions, limiting model interpretability. Similarly, text generation metrics like ROUGE and BLEU focus on n-gram overlap, missing the semantic depth of responses. While advanced metrics like BERTScore attempt to address this, they still fall short in evaluating reasoning. Human evaluation, though ideal for nuanced tasks, is costly and impractical at scale. Moreover, current benchmarks do not adequately measure task complexity, making it hard to gauge whether LLMs are handling complex reasoning or simple tasks. 
### Research work done by others
Multi-hop benchmarks evaluate a model’s ability to connect multiple pieces of information across different sources to answer complex questions, which is critical in medical tasks like clinical decision-making, patient-trial matching. In healthcare, multi-step reasoning is often necessary to integrate various patient data, clinical guidelines, and medical histories to arrive at a diagnosis or treatment plan. However, many existing medical benchmarks fail to fully capture this complexity. For instance, MedHop focuses on multi-hop question-answering in the biomedical domain but lacks detailed annotations of the intermediate reasoning steps that lead to the final answer. Similarly, PubMedQA and BioASQ provide question-answering tasks based on biomedical literature but primarily target single-hop reasoning, without addressing the multi-step reasoning process critical for nuanced medical decision-making.
To address this gap, there is a need for benchmarks that not only evaluate the final answers but also track the reasoning paths taken by models to arrive at those answers. This is where the concept of entailment trees becomes essential. An entailment tree [] represents the multi-hop reasoning process by breaking it down into smaller, verifiable steps that ultimately lead to a conclusion. Entailment trees provide transparency in the decision-making process, allowing researchers to understand how a model connects intermediate steps to arrive at a final answer. By applying entailment trees, multi-hop benchmarks can more accurately assess the complexity and interpretability of a model's reasoning, which is especially important in high-stakes fields like healthcare.
For example, the DiReCT benchmark [] uses entailment trees to model the diagnostic reasoning process from clinical observations to final diagnoses, capturing each step along the way. Similarly, NLI4CT [] focuses on multi-evidence inference in clinical trial reports, requiring models to gather and reason over multiple pieces of evidence, although it does not use detailed intermediate annotations like entailment trees. These examples highlight the growing recognition of the need for benchmarks that assess not just the final outcome but also the reasoning complexity, making entailment trees a crucial tool for evaluating LLMs in healthcare.
Integrating LLMs, with symbolic reasoning systems represents a promising approach to overcoming the limitations of LLMs in complex logical reasoning tasks. LLMs excel at natural language processing but often struggle with logical consistency, particularly in multi-step reasoning. By combining them with symbolic solvers [], this hybrid approach leverages the language understanding capabilities of LLMs while ensuring logical rigor and consistency. For instance, LeanReasoner employs the Lean theorem-proving framework to formalize reasoning tasks, improving accuracy on benchmarks by enforcing strict logical consistency[]. Similarly, LINC combines LLMs with first-order logic provers, where the LLM translates natural language into formal logic, and the prover handles deductive reasoning​[]. LLM-ARC takes this further with an Actor-Critic model, where the critic evaluates logic programs generated by the LLM, offering iterative feedback for continuous improvement[]. Lastly, SymBa integrates symbolic backward chaining, allowing LLMs to decompose complex queries into subgoals, enhancing structured reasoning and proof accuracy​[].

By breaking down the reasoning process into intermediate steps, it becomes easier to pinpoint where logical inconsistencies arise, providing actionable insights that can refine both LLM performance and its integration with symbolic reasoning systems. This structured approach not only helps in identifying errors but also facilitates iterative improvement, ensuring that the model adheres to both natural language understanding and formal logical consistency.

### 2b. Research Plan and Methodology
The proposed benchmark is designed to address critical gaps in the current landscape of medical AI by introducing multi-hop question-answering tasks focused on cardiovascular and neurology domains. These tasks will initially emphasize widely used Clinical Assessment Tools (CATs), like the NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and the Framingham Risk Score, as well as patient-trial matching based on clinical notes. The goal is to evaluate the ability of LLMs to handle complex medical reasoning in these high-impact areas, pushing beyond simple extraction tasks to real-world clinical decision-making challenges.
The benchmark will involve a combination of real-life and synthetic clinical notes, ensuring diversity in the data. Synthetic notes will be generated to fill gaps and ensure representation of varied clinical cases. Additionally, systematic augmentation of the clinical notes will introduce challenges such as obfuscation, irrelevant details, and complex medical terminology. This ensures the LLM’s adaptability to ambiguous, noisy data while simulating real-world clinical variability.
Once tasks are defined, the focus will shift to entailment tree construction. Each task (a medical query paired with a clinical note) will be broken down into structured, intermediate reasoning steps, providing transparency in the decision-making process. These steps will then be translated into formal logic, which allows for more structured reasoning. The final step in the pipeline is to connect the formal logic statements to an external symbolic solver, enabling verification and refinement of the reasoning process, which LLMs alone are often not well-suited to handle.
The benchmark also aims to explore performance optimization through a combination of fine-tuning and prompt-engineering. While fine-tuning will focus on improving the LLM’s ability to formulate problems (i.e., constructing entailment trees and translating them into formal logic), prompt engineering will resolve issues such as knowledge conflicts and arithmetic errors.
The uniqueness of this proposal lies in its medical focus and its integration of entailment trees with an external symbolic solver—a combination not seen in existing benchmarks. Previous models like Lean, LINC, LLM-ARC, and SymBa focus on symbolic reasoning but do not address medical tasks. Similarly, while medical benchmarks like NLI4CT and DiReCT provide valuable insights into clinical reasoning and multi-hop question-answering, they are not connected to external symbolic solvers. This proposal aims to bridge that gap, leveraging symbolic solvers to enhance the rigor and reliability of clinical decision-making tasks.

#### Task2. Clinical note augmentation
In this proposal, an information extraction (multi-hop) task consists of a medical query (such as CATs extraction or patient-trial matching) paired with a clinical note. Once clinical notes and medical queries have been collected, we will focus on rule-based augmentation of these notes. This process is designed to test the adaptability of LLMs when facing varying levels of complexity and ambiguity in clinical documentation.

The **rule-based augmentation** will introduce systematic variations into the clinical notes, to simulate real-life challenges such as indirect data presentations, irrelevant information, and variations in medical terminology. This approach ensures that the benchmark tests the model’s ability to generalize and adapt to diverse clinical note structures. By applying systematic augmentation, we can control the complexity of the extraction task as well as identify which types of reasoning the LLM is more likely to struggle with, offering insight into the model’s performance at different stages of the reasoning process.

The augmentation techniques will include, but are not limited to, indirect data presentation, where clinical information may be provided in ranges, requiring the model to perform conversions or calculations to extract meaningful data. Additionally, models may need to infer implicit information from symptom descriptions or embedded lab results, as this information may not be explicitly stated. To further challenge the model's focus, irrelevant or distracting details will be introduced, testing its ability to prioritize relevant data. Advanced medical terminology, incorporating less common medical terms, will also be used to assess the model's comprehension of complex language. These are just a few approaches, and additional techniques may be introduced as needed.

Each **augmented clinical note** will be labeled based on the original note and the specific type of augmentation it underwent. This labeling will allow for detailed tracking and analysis of how different augmentations affect the model's reasoning capabilities. Moreover, the augmentation will be reflected in the corresponding **entailment tree** for each task. This ensures that the reasoning modifications introduced by augmentation are accurately captured in the multi-step reasoning annotations.

The rule-based augmentation will provide additional flexibility by allowing us to annotation the entailment tree of a task with relative ease, as we will have a clear understanding of how each augmentation affects the reasoning process. This capability enables efficient testing of different reasoning pathways and allows for more targeted exploration of the model's reasoning strengths and weaknesses.

#### Task 3. Task decomposition and translation in Formal Logic
The goal of this task is to convert medical queries paired with clinical notes into **entailment trees** and formal logic representations. This process will break down the reasoning paths required for each task into smaller, manageable steps, ensuring transparency in the decision-making process and facilitating formal validation using symbolic reasoning systems.

The first phase of this task involves the **decomposition** of medical tasks into an **entailment tree**. Each node in the entailment tree will represent an intermediate step of the reasoning process, annotated with the relevant clinical evidence or knowledge required to move from one step to the next. For example, in a task where the query asks whether a patient meets the inclusion criteria for a clinical trial, the entailment tree would decompose the reasoning into individual steps: checking age eligibility, comorbidity exclusions, medication history, and other clinical factors. Each of these steps would be a node in the tree, annotated with the corresponding data from the clinical note.

Once the entailment tree is constructed, the next phase is the translation of each node and reasoning step into a formal logic representation. This formalization will be done using frameworks like First-Order Logic (FOL) or Answer Set Programming (ASP). The formal logic representation will capture the medical entities, relationships, and conditions needed for each reasoning step, enabling the task to be processed by symbolic solvers such as Prover9[] or Lean[].

The translation of medical queries and clinical notes into **entailment trees** and **formal logic** presents inherent challenges, particularly due to the varying complexity of reasoning steps involved. Some medical tasks are straightforward and rule-based, while others involve uncertainty, implicit information, or probabilistic reasoning []. These factors significantly complicate the process of constructing a formal logic representation. 
We will broadly classify the tasks in two classes well-structured versus complex, uncertain:
1. In well-structured cases, the entailment tree will consist of direct logical steps, where each node is a clear rule or condition that can be converted into formal logic. 
2. For tasks involving uncertainty or implicit reasoning, the entailment tree will incorporate probabilistic nodes or nodes that represent inferred information. These trees may require more advanced formal representations, such as probabilistic logic, and could be refined iteratively using feedback loops from reasoning critics like those in [LLM-ARC] .

#### Task 4. Investigating LLM behavior and performance optimization
The final task in this project aims to leverage the dataset developed in the earlier stages to both **learn about the behavior of the LLM** and **optimize its performance**. By analyzing how the LLM processes medical queries and clinical notes in the benchmark, we can gain insights into its strengths and weaknesses, while refining its performance through **fine-tuning** and **prompt-engineering** techniques.

The dataset created in Tasks 1–3 will allow us to analyze how the LLM handles different types of medical reasoning tasks, particularly multi-hop reasoning over complex, augmented clinical notes. Key areas for analysis include:
1. **Task Complexity and Performance**: By examining how the LLM performs on simple versus complex tasks (e.g., direct queries vs. tasks with implicit reasoning or uncertainty), we can gain insights into which areas the model excels in and where it struggles. This will inform the development of more effective fine-tuning strategies.
2. **Error Categorization and Diagnostics**: By analyzing the LLM’s mistakes—whether in **entailment tree construction**, **translation into formal logic**, or final answers—we can better understand where the reasoning process breaks down. These diagnostic insights will help guide the refinement of the model through more targeted interventions.
3. **Impact of Augmentation**: Since the dataset includes augmented clinical notes, we can study how different types of augmentations (e.g., data obfuscation, irrelevant details, indirect data) affect the LLM’s reasoning capabilities. This will give us deeper insights into how well the model handles real-world variability and noise.

Given the nature of LLMs, they may not be best suited for rigorous, complex reasoning tasks that involve uncertainty or probabilistic rules. Therefore, rather than focusing on solving these highly complex reasoning problems directly, our plan is to explore whether fine-tuning can improve the LLM's ability to **formulate problems**—specifically in terms of **entailment tree construction** and their **subsequent translation into formal logic**.

Since most reasoning will be handled at the problem formulation stage, prompt engineering will be used to ensure the LLM can handle errors related to domain-specific knowledge, arithmetic, and other inconsistencies. As we analyze the LLM’s performance on the benchmark, the scope of prompt engineering will be refined based on observed patterns of errors.
