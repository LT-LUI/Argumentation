Zeba Khan’s opening statement in *Is Islam a Religion of Peace?* Select a passage to explore its role in the argument.
I want to express my deepest gratitude to Mr. Rosenkrantz and the Intelligence Squared Forum for allowing me to speak tonight alongside such well known and far more distinguished co panelists. This is particularly an honor for me because let's be honest, I haven't written a book. I'm not a regular on national TV or radio. What I have is my story. I am a Muslim American woman born and raised in Toledo, Ohio by two very loving Indian Muslim parents. My sister, brother and I were raised in a middle class American home. We went to mosque on Sundays, attended Sunday school classes and prayed the community prayer with our community of Pakistanis, Lebanese and Syrian Muslims. When I was in high school, our mosque president was a woman who did not wear a headscarf. And it may come as a surprise to some of you, but for the entirety of my life, men and women have prayed side by side at our mosque, and both can enter the prayer hall using the same door. My parents are both very religious people, but they express their faith in different ways. My father emphasizes the devotional and he tends to spend his time praying and reciting the Quran, whereas my mother emphasizes a more constructive approach. She uses community service and volunteering to express hers. But what they both share is fundamental Islamic principles. First and foremost, seek knowledge. They sent. They urged their children, all three of us, to question, to have critical minds and to doubt. They wanted us to engage fully with our faith and to question everything. They lived out the Quranic commandment that there is no compulsion in religion and also that God said in the Quran, I made you into many tribes so that you might know one another. And as such, they enrolled me and my siblings in a Hebrew day school for nine years, where we learned Hebrew, read the Torah, and prayed in a synagogue almost every morning. They always wanted us to learn about other faiths, and they always made sure that we knew the difference, though, between Islam and Judaism. But they always made sure we also respected our Jewish sisters and brothers in faith.

> So how then do we reconcile these seemingly contradictory verses? How then do we decide whether Islam is a religion of peace?
>
> <span id="zk1-criterion" data-layers="Argument Definition">The only way to answer that question is, is to take an honest look at the people who practice the faith and how they interpret it.</span>

> [!reader-view] Standard form
> - **Premise 1.** Khan identifies divergent messages in scripture. [Text](#zk1-scripture)
> - **Implicit premise.** When scripture points in different directions, selected verses alone cannot settle the character of the religion.
> - **Intermediate conclusion.** Examine how people interpret and practise their faith. Khan’s phrase “the only way” makes a stronger claim than the premises establish. [Text](#zk1-criterion)
> - **Empirical premise.** Khan presents Gallup’s classification as evidence that most Muslims are peaceful. Whether the survey measured peaceful conduct is a separate question. [Text](#zk1-survey)
> - **Implicit premise.** Predominantly peaceful practice is sufficient to characterize the religion as peaceful.
> - **Conclusion.** On that criterion, Islam qualifies as a religion of peace.

> [!reader-view] Toulmin
> - **Grounds.** Khan appeals to a survey of Muslims and interprets the majority category as peaceful. [Text](#zk1-survey)
> - **Warrant.** A religion can be judged by how its adherents interpret and practise it. [Text](#zk1-criterion)
> - **Backing offered for the warrant.** The variety of scriptural passages motivates looking at how believers interpret them. [Text](#zk1-scripture)
> - **Implicit step.** The majority’s practice can stand for the religion as a whole.
> - **Claim.** Islam is a religion of peace.

> [!reader-view] Alternative readings
> - **Exclusive criterion.** “The only way” suggests that lived practice settles the question. A critic could accept the survey premise and still reject this criterion. [Text](#zk1-criterion)
> - **Necessary evidence.** A narrower reading is that any adequate answer must consider believers’ practice. This leaves room for texts and institutions to count as well. [Text](#zk1-criterion)
