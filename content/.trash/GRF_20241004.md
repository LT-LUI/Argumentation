### Background
(**Advances in LLM**)
Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a transformative force in artificial intelligence, demonstrating remarkable capabilities across diverse domains. These models, trained on vast corpora of text data, have evolved from simple statistical approaches to sophisticated neural architectures capable of understanding context, generating human-like text, and performing complex reasoning tasks. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The application of LLMs on various domains.</mark> These advancements have led to unprecedented performance in natural language processing tasks, including text generation, translation, and question-answering.

(**Application of LLM in medicine**)
Large language models (LLMs) are making significant strides in healthcare. In medical diagnosis, LLMs can analyze patient symptoms, medical records, and clinical data to assist in identifying illnesses such as COVID-19, Alzheimer's, and dementia [50-56]. These models also support clinical decision-making, trial recruitment, and patient education. For patient care, LLMs offer personalized treatment recommendations, continuous monitoring, and tailored interventions that improve outcomes by enabling data-driven decisions [61-66]. This technology allows healthcare providers to deliver precise, patient-centric care. LLMs also play a key role in clinical decision support by analyzing vast medical datasets and providing evidence-based recommendations in fields like radiology, oncology, and dermatology [67-69].




(**Performance improvement**)
OpenAI's latest model [ChatGPT-o1](2024 A Preliminary Study of o1 in Medicine: Are We Closer to an AI Doctor) achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of medical benchmarks, achieved an accuracy of 74.3% across 19 medical datasets. Despite the impressive results, it was shown by the authors that it still suffers from hallucination and memory recall issues suggesting further research is required. Additionally, although proprietary large language models (LLMs) dominate performance on the majority of benchmarks [2024 Large Language Models in Healthcare and Medical Domain_ A Review], <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">the sensitivity and priority of data privacy in medical data provide strong incentives to improve the performance of open-source LLMs.</mark> Enhancing open-source models would allow for greater transparency, customization, and adherence to privacy regulations, enabling wider adoption in healthcare applications.
Apart from developing more advanced foundational models, fine-tuning and prompt engineering are two of the most common techniques to improve LLM performance.
- Fine-Tuning: Fine-tuning involves training the foundational model with task- or domain-specific data, updating the model parameters to adapt to specific applications. This process customizes the LLM to better understand the nuances and terminologies of a particular field. For example, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) tuning fine-tunes the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps, enhancing its problem-solving abilities and interpretability.
- Prompt Engineering: Prompt engineering, or in-context learning, improves the performance of the model by carefully designing the input prompts provided to the LLM instead of updating the model parameters. By structuring prompts effectively, the model can be guided to produce more accurate and relevant responses. Some of the prompt engineering techniques include,  Reflection - Encouraging the model to review and refine its own outputs to reduce errors,  Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting: Prompting the model to generate step-by-step reasoning leading to the final answer, decomposition - Breaking down complex tasks into simpler sub-tasks that the model can solve sequentially.

Reasoning and domain knowledge are two key abilities of LLMs that are essential for solving various tasks. While LLMs display impressive flexibility in addressing diverse problems, they are often implemented as part of larger applications. In such cases, other abilities—such as instruction-following, function-calling, retrieval-augmented generation, and resolving knowledge conflicts within specific contexts—also need to be evaluated.

(**Role of benchmark in LLM research:**)
Benchmarks are essential tools in developing LLM medical applications. They provide standardized measures to evaluate and compare model performance for various tasks, such as clinical information extraction, diagnosis support, and interpreting patient data. [2024 A Comprehensive Survey on Evaluating Large Language Model Applications in the Medical Industry] They enable objective comparisons between proprietary and open-source models. Moreover, benchmarks ensure that enhancements in LLMs translate into real-world applicability, verifying that models can handle the nuances and complexities of medical information to effectively support healthcare professionals. They foster innovation and collaboration within the medical AI community, ultimately advancing the development of reliable, accurate LLMs that can improve patient outcomes.

Other than evaluating the models ability to perform a specific task, benchmarks can also reveal key LLM behaviours. For instance, in an application that utilize the retrieval augment generation method, where task specific or up-to-date information is retrieved from external sources and provided to the LLM as supporting information. When there is a knowledge conflict, i.e. when the provided information is different to the parameterized internal knowledge, the ClashEval dataset demonstrated how likely the LLMs use the provide information is affected by how confident they are associated with that piece of information. These insights can are crucial to develop better LLM applications, and can guide researchers in refining models through fine-tuning and prompt engineering techniques.

### Research work done by others
(**Medical LLM benchmark overview**)
Many benchmarks have been created to assess LLM performance across various medical tasks. For general evaluation across multiple medical domains, MultiMedQA integrates several large datasets, such as PubMedQA, MedQA, MedMCQA, and MMLU. MedQA, a large-scale benchmark with 15,908 USMLE-style questions, focuses on testing LLMs across areas like pathology, pharmacology, and anatomy. MedMCQA, with 194,000 questions, aims to evaluate LLMs in medical contexts, simulating real-world clinical reasoning.

In addition to general medical knowledge, LLMs are also applied in medical text summarization. The MEDIQA 2021 challenge introduced datasets such as MeQSum, MEDIQA-AnS, and radiology reports from Indiana University and Stanford, serving as benchmarks for summarizing consumer health queries, aggregating answers, and summarizing radiology impressions. Evaluation tools included metrics like ROUGE-2, BERTScore, and HOLMS.

Moreover, LLMs show strong performance in extracting structured data from complex medical texts, such as through named entity recognition, relation extraction, and event extraction. For evaluating these capabilities, benchmarks need to be built around tasks like entity linking and PICO extraction. The review paper **"2024: A Comprehensive Survey on Evaluating Large Language Model Applications in the Medical Industry"** provides an extensive review of these evaluation efforts.

While current benchmarks provide an effective means to evaluate the ability of LLMs in addressing real-world medical tasks, they present significant limitations. These limitations become particularly pronounced when seeking to gain deeper insights into LLM behavior—insights crucial for further model improvement through fine-tuning and prompt engineering.
1. **Multiple-Choice Question Benchmarks**  
    Although multiple-choice question (MCQ) benchmarks, such as those in MedQA and MedMCQA, offer the advantage of easy performance evaluation, they fall short in assessing the reasoning behind LLM decision-making. This lack of insight is a barrier to enhancing model interpretability. Without understanding the reasoning process, users may struggle to place confidence in LLM applications.
2. **Text Generation Metrics**  
    For benchmarks that evaluate free-text responses, such as summarization tasks. Established techniques such as BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR, which focus on n-gram overlap, fail to capture the true semantic meaning of medical text. These metrics primarily assess textual similarity between LLM-generated responses and reference texts, yet overlook deeper reasoning processes. While more advanced metrics like BERTScore attempt to capture semantic similarities by comparing contextual embeddings, they still fall short of assessing the model’s reasoning capabilities. 
3. **Human Evaluation**  
    Human evaluation remains the gold standard for assessing LLM-generated content, especially in nuanced fields like medicine where expertise is crucial. However, large-scale manual evaluation is labor-intensive and cost-prohibitive. Given the specialized knowledge required, conducting human evaluations at scale is not a viable long-term solution for continuous benchmarking, particularly as models and tasks grow in complexity.
4. **Task Complexity and Difficulty Grading**  
    Current benchmarks do not adequately define or measure the complexity and difficulty of the tasks they evaluate. As a result, it is difficult to discern whether LLM performance reflects genuine reasoning or is simply the result of handling relatively simple, lower-order tasks. A well-structured difficulty gradient across benchmarks is essential for evaluating the progression of LLM capabilities, especially in the context of high-level medical reasoning.
    
To address these limitations, future benchmarks must evolve to offer greater insight into the reasoning processes of LLMs. This evolution is essential not only for evaluating LLM performance but for developing more transparent and reliable LLM applications.

In the context of benchmarks that evaluate free-text responses, such as summarization tasks, recent advancements in LLMs have sparked considerable interest in their ability to self-evaluate the quality of their outputs, potentially reducing the need for human experts. For instance, frameworks like **Reflexion** and **Self-refine** propose prompting LLMs multiple times to generate more refined responses. Reflexion simulates human-like learning by encouraging AI agents to reflect on their mistakes and improve over time, while Self-refine prompts the model for initial output, feedback, and subsequent refinement. Although there is growing support for the idea that LLMs can assess their own outputs, this self-correction approach has its limitations. As highlighted in the literature, this strategy may not be universally applicable, as LLMs are ultimately constrained by their internal knowledge and reasoning abilities​.

Moreover, there are frameworks aimed at evaluating the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, such as **NPHardEval** and **DyVal**, which dynamically adjust task difficulty based on complexity classes. These approaches focus on more structured, well-defined problems like arithmetic and algorithmic reasoning, where deductive steps are clearer. However, these benchmarks may not adequately capture the nuances and complexities inherent in free-text medical tasks, where reasoning is often less structured and more context-dependent. Expanding on this, benchmarks in the medical field must evolve to include dynamic evaluation systems like DyVal, but with a focus on reasoning tasks specific to clinical and biomedical contexts.
 
With the advancement of LLM, considerable effort has be spent on investigating LLMs' ability to evaluate the quality of its own output, thus potentially replacing human experts. For example, frameworks such as Reflexion, Self-refine suggested to prompt the LLM multiple times in order to generate better responses. Reflexion creates AI agents that learn by reflecting on failures and enhancing their results, much like humans do. Self-refine prompts the LLM in three ways: (1) for initial generation, (2) for feed-back, and (3) for refinement.  [While there seems to be a consensus that LLMs can assess their own outputs, but this approach may not be suitable for all cases.](When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.01297) [Additionally, there are reports that this approach is still bottlenecked by the models' internal knowledge and ability.](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.27/)(2024 Automatically Correcting Large Language Models: Surveying the Landscape of Diverse Automated Correction Strategies)

### 2b. Research Plan and Methodology
In this proposal, we aim to create a medical benchmark that not only evaluates the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) but also facilitates the investigation of LLM behaviors and prompt engineering algorithms. We intend to develop a question-answering benchmark focused on extracting information from clinical notes.

In the context of information extraction from clinical notes, the difficulty of a medical query depends not only on the query itself but also on the specific clinical note from which the LLM is extracting information. For instance, if we want to determine the New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Classification for Heart Failure of a patient, this information might be explicitly stated in some clinical notes, making it a straightforward extraction task. However, in other notes, the LLM may need to infer the classification based on symptoms described, involving a more complex reasoning process. Therefore, the challenge for the LLM depends on both the medical task and the nuances of the specific clinical note.

For the detailed design of the benchmark, we draw inspiration from two recent publications: **"Disentangling Logic: The Role of Context in Large Language Model Reasoning Capabilities"** (2024) and **"Logic-LM: Empowering Large Language Models with Symbolic Solvers for Faithful Logical Reasoning"** (2023). Based on the DyVal framework, _Disentangling Logic_ introduced the ContextHub benchmark, where tree-structured logic templates are generated with each node representing a subproblem. These templates are then contextualized into domain-specific reasoning tasks. This approach allowed the authors to compare LLMs’ performance on identical logical constructs in both abstract and richly contextualized settings.

Following the direction of ContextHub, we aim to implement a similar approach in a realistic clinical setting. Instead of using generated logic templates, our templates will be based on real medical queries, such as determining the NYHA classification. Once we have these templates, we can annotate each node accordingly. Besides incorporating various levels of difficulty, we will augment the clinical notes in multiple ways to reflect different real-life variations, systematically testing the LLM's ability to handle these nuances. The node-level annotations will be valuable for researchers and developers to identify and categorize errors, facilitating targeted improvements in fine-tuning and prompt engineering.

Another component of the benchmark is inspired by the concept of Logic-LM, where the authors propose utilizing LLMs to translate natural language problems into symbolic formulations. A deterministic symbolic solver is then used to perform inference on the formulated problem. In this project, we will generate symbolic formulations for each of the clinical extraction tasks. These symbolic representations will evaluate the LLM's ability to translate natural language problems into symbolic form. Additionally, this dataset can be used to fine-tune the LLM for improved performance in this type of translation task.

We aim to develop a question-answering benchmark with the following properties:
1. Realistic Clinical Scenarios: Medical queries and clinical notes that closely resemble real clinical situations.
2. Variable Query Complexity: Medical queries with varying levels of complexity.
3. Augmented Clinical Notes: Clinical notes with different types and levels of augmentation to imitate diverse real-life situations.
4. Disentangled Reasoning Steps: For each task (query with the corresponding note), the logical reasoning steps and medical knowledge are disentangled.
5. Intermediate Annotations: Provide intermediate annotations for each logical reasoning step.
6. Complexity Labels: Label each task with its corresponding complexity.
7. Symbolic Representations: Include symbolic representations of the tasks.
