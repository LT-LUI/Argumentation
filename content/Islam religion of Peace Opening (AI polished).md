#### 2. Is the disagreement ontological rather than empirical?
This raises the possibility that the debate cannot be resolved merely by accumulating more evidence.
Suppose we establish beyond reasonable doubt that:
- Most Muslims are peaceful.
- Islamic texts contain both peaceful and violent passages.
- Some Islamic legal traditions are incompatible with modern liberal values.
- Certain religious authorities and political institutions promote intolerant interpretations.
- Political and historical factors contribute to contemporary religious extremism.
Even if all sides accepted these observations, they might still disagree about whether Islam is a religion of peace.
Why?
Because they may be applying different definitions of religion and different standards for determining what makes a religion peaceful.
Under a sociological definition, the peaceful behaviour of the majority could be decisive.
Under an essentialist or textual definition, the content and authority of the foundational teachings might carry greater weight.
Under an institutional definition, the practices and influence of religious authorities and governing institutions might be more important.
This suggests that **the disagreement may be partly conceptual rather than purely factual**.
#### 4. Preliminary assessment
Murray's strongest contribution may be his distinction between the foundational, institutional, and contemporary dimensions of Islam.
This framework allows him to acknowledge the peaceful majority without accepting that Islam must therefore be a religion of peace.
However, his argument still faces several unresolved questions:
- Does the presence of violence in religious texts or history necessarily prevent a religion from being considered peaceful?
- How much contemporary violence can be causally attributed to religious teachings?
- Why should influential religious institutions carry greater representational weight than ordinary believers?
- What standard should we use to combine the different dimensions of a religion into an overall judgement?
At this stage, I find Murray's framework useful, but I am not convinced that he has established a sufficiently clear or consistent standard for concluding that Islam is not a religion of peace.
More importantly, **the framework reinforces my suspicion that the central difficulty lies in the definition and representation of religion itself**.
Perhaps the most useful outcome of this debate is not deciding whether Islam is peaceful, but recognising why the apparently simple question is so difficult to answer.
